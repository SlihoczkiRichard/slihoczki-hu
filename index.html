I work on network security, mostly Zero Trust. The idea that every request should be checked, no matter where it comes from, is almost fifty years old. What's new is that we can finally afford to do it everywhere.

I've configured HPE Aruba Security Service Edge policies and zero-trust tunnels on real enterprise networks. C:\> tracert 1975Reading the past

Zero Trust sounds new. It isn't. Following an idea back to where it started is how I make sense of where it's going.

  1. 1975

    Saltzer and Schroeder write down the principles of least privilege and complete mediation: check every access, every time.

  2. 1988

    The Morris worm spreads across the early Internet. Trusting a machine because it's "inside" stops looking like a safe bet.

  3. 2004

    The Jericho Forum argues for de-perimeterisation. The network edge is no longer a wall you can rely on.

  4. 2010

    John Kindervag at Forrester gives the idea a name: Zero Trust.

  5. 2014

    Google describes BeyondCorp: no privileged corporate network, access decided per request.

  6. 2020

    NIST SP 800-207 turns it into a reference architecture.

  7. 2026

    My thesis: once everyone deploys it, what happens under load, during scale-out, and when a connector quietly disappears? █

THESIS BSc · BME

Empirical Evaluation of Performance, Scalability, and Threat Protection in Connector-Based Zero Trust Architecture

In HPE Aruba SSE, private applications are reached through connectors: small VMs that open outbound mTLS tunnels to the cloud edge, so nothing on the inside listens for incoming connections. I built a two-host ESXi testbed around them and ran four experiments: how much load one connector takes, how fast a second one can be switched on automatically, what the client sees when a connector silently drops off the network, and how quickly a threat-intel update turns into a block.

RESULTS.CSV
E1 loadone connector (2 vCPU, 4 GB) peaks at 3,888 req/s with 1,400 concurrent clients; p50 309 ms, p95 609 ms. Past that, throughput collapses.
E2 scale-outCPU-triggered power-on via the vCenter API: VM up in ~5 s, carrying traffic in ~1.5 min. It replaced the first connector instead of sharing load.
E3 failurea silent network loss on the active connector meant a full outage of ~5 min (median 301.5 s) before traffic moved over.
E4 threat intelURLhaus indicators synced into an SWG category; blocking took effect in ~1 s or after 10+ min, two distinct ranges, cause still open.
